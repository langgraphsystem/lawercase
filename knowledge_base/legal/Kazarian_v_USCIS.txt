CASE NAME: Kazarian v. USCIS
CITATION: 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010)
DATE: March 4, 2010

SUMMARY:
Kazarian v. USCIS is the seminal Ninth Circuit decision that established the current two-step framework for adjudicating EB-1A (Alien of Extraordinary Ability) petitions. The court held that USCIS may not unilaterally impose novel evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth in the regulations.

THE TWO-STEP FRAMEWORK:

STEP 1: EVIDENTIARY CRITERIA (Counting the Evidence)
- The adjudicator must determine whether the petitioner has submitted evidence that meets at least three of the ten regulatory criteria found at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).
- Alternatively, the petitioner may submit a one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award).
- CRITICAL HOLDING: At this step, USCIS cannot impose "extra-regulatory" requirements. For example:
    - If the regulation requires "authorship of scholarly articles," USCIS cannot require those articles to be "widely cited" at Step 1.
    - If the regulation requires "participation as a judge of the work of others," USCIS cannot require that the judging be for "major" competitions at Step 1.
- If the petitioner meets fewer than three criteria (and lacks a major award), the petition is denied.
- If the petitioner meets at least three criteria, the adjudication proceeds to Step 2.

STEP 2: FINAL MERITS DETERMINATION (Totality of the Evidence)
- The adjudicator must consider all of the evidence in its totality to determine whether the petitioner has:
    1. A level of expertise indicating that the individual is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor.
    2. Sustained national or international acclaim.
- It is at this step that the *quality* and *significance* of the evidence are evaluated.
- For example, while low citation counts cannot disqualify an article at Step 1, they can be used at Step 2 to argue that the petitioner is not at the "very top of the field."

COMMON USCIS ERRORS (DEFENSE STRATEGIES):
1. Circular Reasoning (Merging Steps): USCIS often improperly imports Step 2 analysis into Step 1. (e.g., "You judged a competition, but it wasn't prestigious enough, so it doesn't count for Step 1.") This is a direct violation of Kazarian.
2. Novel Evidentiary Requirements: Requiring evidence not listed in the regulations (e.g., requiring a specific salary amount when the regulation only says "high salary").
3. Failure to Consider Totality: In Step 2, USCIS must look at the whole picture. They cannot simply dismiss each piece of evidence individually; they must weigh the cumulative effect.

RELEVANCE TO EB-1A PETITIONS:
Every EB-1A petition letter should explicitly structure its argument around this two-step framework to prevent the adjudicator from making legal errors. The "Preponderance of the Evidence" standard applies to both steps.
